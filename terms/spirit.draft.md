# Soul and Spirit

Scripture quotations are ESV unless another translation is named; bold emphasis is added. The Bible uses *soul* and *spirit* in overlapping, context-dependent ways. A soul may be a living creature, a person, a life, or an expression of desire. Spirit may refer to wind, breath, an inner disposition, the Father’s Spirit, or a personal agent. Here, *spirit-being* means an ordinarily unseen personal agent identified by speech or action in an applicable narrative—not everything called a spirit. No universal “immaterial being” definition fits these uses, and the passages supply no materiality test.

## Soul

### Greek mythology and soul

In [Homer’s *Odyssey*, book 11](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.11.xi.html), the dead appear in a story of the underworld. Odysseus tries to embrace his mother, but *“each time she flitted from my embrace as it were a dream or phantom.”* She says that *“the soul flits away as though it were a dream.”* This is evidence for a portrayal of the dead in Greek epic, not for what biblical authors meant by *soul*.

[Plato’s *Phaedo*](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/phaedo.html) asks whether death is *“the separation of soul and body”* and discusses purification in those terms. That is a philosophical argument, distinct even from Homer’s narrative. Neither work can supply a default meaning for Hebrew *nephesh* or settle the meaning of Greek *psychē* in a biblical sentence.

### Soul dictionaries

The [Hebrew *nephesh*](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/5315.htm) has a range that includes living being, life, self, person, desire, and emotion. The [Greek *psychē*](https://biblehub.com/greek/5590.htm) likewise occurs in contexts concerning life and persons as well as what English translations call *soul*. These are ranges of usage, not a list of properties possessed by every referent. A word used for both animals and the Father need not describe the same kind of existence in each case.

A lexicon helps identify possible senses; the sentence and its setting determine which sense is at issue. Theological claims attached to a dictionary entry, including claims that every soul is naturally immortal, require their own biblical evidence. The examples below therefore begin with the text rather than with a model of human parts.

### Biblical soul usage with quoted examples

Genesis 2:7 says of the man:

> “the man became a **living creature**.”

Here *nephesh* describes the living creature the man became. It does not say that an independently conscious object was put inside him. The same Hebrew expression appears with animals in Genesis 1:20 and 1:24, so its presence alone cannot identify a uniquely human faculty.

Other contexts speak of a person’s life. Genesis 35:18 describes Rachel’s soul departing as she died, while 1 Kings 17:21–22 describes a child’s life returning when he revived. Those accounts connect *nephesh* with life and death; they do not, by themselves, describe everything that happened to Rachel while dead. Even translation varies with context: Numbers 6:6 prohibits approaching a “dead body,” using *nephesh* for the dead person in that instruction.

Matthew 10:28 makes a distinction that should not be erased:

> “those who kill the **body** but cannot kill the **soul**”; “him who can destroy both **soul** and **body** in hell.”

In this warning, *soul* is not simply interchangeable with *body*. Equally, the prospect that both can be destroyed does not establish an inherently indestructible soul. Read the distinction as part of Jesus’ warning, without requiring every use of *psychē* to name a separate substance.

### Animal soul

The Father’s creation speech in Genesis 1:20 calls for waters to swarm with “**living creatures**.” Genesis 1:21 speaks of “every **living creature** that moves,” and Genesis 1:24 calls forth land “**living creatures** according to their kinds.” These expressions use *nephesh*; animal life is not an exception to the word’s biblical range. Genesis 2:19 likewise says “every **living creature**” when describing the animals brought before the man. The shared designation is evidence that humans are not the only creatures described this way, not evidence that every creature has the same capacities or destiny.

The surrounding account treats animals as recipients of care, not as an incidental word-study example. Genesis 1:22 says, “God **blessed** them,” addressing the creatures’ fruitfulness. Genesis 1:29–30 assigns food to humans and animals; the latter receive “every **green plant for food**.” Genesis 1:30 also calls the animals those with “the **breath of life**,” although the relevant Hebrew expression there belongs to the *nephesh* vocabulary, not to a proof that *ruach* occurs in that verse. Genesis 1:31 calls the completed creation “**very good**.” Blessing and provision show the Father’s regard for living creatures; they do not establish animal moral agency.

The same regard continues after the flood. The covenant in Genesis 9:10 includes “every **living creature** that is with you, the birds, the livestock, and every beast of the earth.” Genesis 9:15–16 again names “every **living creature of all flesh**” in its covenant language. These passages give animals a substantive place in the creation and covenant accounts. They neither deny human distinctions nor teach that an animal soul is an immortal, disembodied person. These creation and covenant details are also covered in the [animals article](https://eternal.family.net.za/creation/animals).

### Human soul

The man “became a **living creature**” in Genesis 2:7; *nephesh* can therefore designate the human being, not merely a hidden possession. In other human settings it concerns life, desire, or the person acting and feeling. Rachel’s death in Genesis 35:18 and the child’s revival in 1 Kings 17:21–23 are examples involving life’s departure and return. Matthew 10:28, by contrast, deliberately mentions soul alongside body. Neither set of passages should cancel the other: a word can have several related uses, and a particular warning can distinguish terms that overlap elsewhere.

Human emotion appears with both terms. Jesus says in John 12:27:

> “Now is my **soul** troubled.”

John 13:21 says:

> “Jesus was troubled in his **spirit**”

These descriptions do not support assigning feeling to soul but excluding it from spirit.

A description of the whole person is a reasonable reading in passages that call a living human a *nephesh*. It is an inference from those passages, not a rule that every reference to a human soul must mean “body plus breath.” The [human soul article](https://eternal.family.net.za/creation/human/soul) collects further examples of life, feeling, and personal reference; those examples must still be read in their respective contexts.

### Angelic soul

The passages considered here do not clearly call an angel a *nephesh* or *psychē*. That limits a proposed biblical definition of “angelic soul”; it does not prove that angels lack personhood or that they possess no life. Scripture can describe a heavenly speaker without applying the word *soul* to that speaker, as in 1 Kings 22:19–22.

Consequently, neither the animal and human uses of *nephesh* nor a Greek account of shades supplies an anatomy for angels. Where the biblical text leaves the category unnamed, the appropriate conclusion is narrower: “angelic soul” is not established by the cited examples.

### Collective soul

Acts 4:32 says:

> “those who believed were of one heart and **soul**.”

The believers remain multiple people. “One soul” expresses their shared allegiance and life together, not a newly formed collective being. First Samuel 18:1–3 likewise describes closeness between Jonathan and David with soul language, while still referring to two persons. Such uses prevent a rigid definition in which every *soul* must be an individually detachable object.

Philippians 1:27 offers a useful comparison with spirit language: believers stand in “one spirit” and strive with “one mind”; the Greek word rendered “mind” there is *psychē*. Translation and context convey their unity without making the community a literal group ghost.

### Soul of God

The Father speaks in Leviticus 26:11:

> “my **soul** shall not abhor you.”

The judgment warning in Leviticus 26:30 says:

> “my **soul** will abhor you.”

These are expressions of the Father’s own acceptance and aversion. Isaiah 42:1 gives another instance in direct speech:

> “my chosen, in whom my **soul** delights; I have put my **Spirit** upon him.”

The verse expresses the Father’s delight in his chosen servant and the placing of his Spirit upon that servant. It does not describe the Father as a mortal breathing creature or assign him the anatomy of Genesis 2:7. Nor should “my soul” and “my Spirit” be treated as interchangeable merely because they occur in one sentence.

## Spirit

### Greek mythology and spirit

Greek material does not present one uniform definition of *spirit*. Homer’s account of a departed soul and Plato’s argument about soul and body concern different questions; neither makes every occurrence of biblical *pneuma* a ghost. A later report of Stoic views in [Diogenes Laertius, *Lives*, book 7](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_VII) says some thinkers defined the soul as *“a warm breath”* and argued that it was bodily and perishable. That reported philosophical position contrasts with an automatically immaterial account of soul or spirit.

These works are historical witnesses to varied Greek discussions. They are not biblical evidence for the nature of the Father’s Spirit, a human spirit, an animal’s breath, or a spirit that speaks in a biblical narrative.

### Spirit dictionaries

The [Hebrew *ruach*](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/7307.htm) ranges across wind, breath, and spirit. A related but distinct Hebrew word, [*neshamah*](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/5397.htm), means breath and occurs in life-and-breath passages such as Genesis 2:7, Genesis 7:22, and Job 27:3. Their overlap does not make them one word or guarantee that either describes a separate being.

Biblical Aramaic also uses [*ruach*](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/7308.htm): Daniel 2:35 uses it for wind, Daniel 5:20 for a proud or hardened spirit, and Daniel 7:15 for Daniel’s anxious spirit. These are examples within the canonical Aramaic text. A cognate from another language or period should not be inserted as though it were an attested Aramaic *nephesh* in those passages.

The [Greek *pneuma*](https://biblehub.com/greek/4151.htm) also has context-sensitive uses involving wind or breath and spirit. Its use for the Father’s Spirit or for a personal speaker cannot be transferred to every use involving air, life, or disposition. Conversely, “wind” is not an adequate definition of a spirit that speaks and acts.

### Biblical spirit usage with quoted examples

The life shared by creatures is one context. The Father announces in Genesis 6:17 that the flood will destroy:

> “all flesh in which is the **breath of life**.”

Genesis 7:15 repeats “all flesh in which there was the **breath of life**.” Both expressions contain *ruach*. Genesis 7:22 says that everything on dry land “in whose nostrils was the **breath of life** died”; its wording brings breath vocabulary together rather than presenting a diagram of separate parts. Breath in these accounts marks living creatures’ dependence on life, not a license to call every use of *ruach* a personal being.

A person’s inner condition is another context. Daniel 7:15 says:

> “my **spirit** within me was anxious.”

This Aramaic sentence reports Daniel’s condition, not a second anxious character inside him. Job 32:8 says:

> “it is the **spirit** in man, the **breath** of the Almighty, that makes him understand.”

Elihu is the speaker in Job 32; the verse is evidence of his expressed understanding, not direct speech from the Father. Job 26:4 illustrates why translation alone cannot settle a word’s sense: the ESV asks “whose **breath** has come out from you?” where the NIV uses “spirit.” The Hebrew word there is *neshamah*.

A narrative can also call a speaking agent a spirit. In Micaiah’s vision, 1 Kings 22:19 places the heavenly host before the Father; verse 21 says:

> “Then a **spirit** came forward and stood before the LORD.”

The spirit speaks and proposes action in verses 21–22. This is more than a change in someone’s mood or a gust of wind. Yet the account does not provide a physical analysis of that spirit or equate every spirit with every member of the heavenly host.

### Animal spirit

Animals are explicitly within passages using *ruach* for the breath of life. Genesis 6:17 and 7:15 say “all flesh,” and the flood account includes animal kinds, not humans alone. Ecclesiastes 3:19 makes the shared mortality explicit:

> “They all have the same **breath**.”

Verse 20 adds:

> “All are from the dust, and to dust all return.”

Ecclesiastes 3:21 then asks:

> “Who knows whether the **spirit** of man goes upward and the **spirit** of the beast goes down into the earth?”

The question is not a declaration that their postmortem destinations differ. These verses rule out the claim that Scripture never uses spirit or breath language of animals. They do not establish that animal and human experience, responsibility, or future must be identical. Genesis 1:22, 1:29–31, and 9:10–16 add the creation, provision, and covenant setting for these living creatures; animal spirit is not a reason to overlook the Father’s stated care for them.

### Human spirit

Human spirit language covers life, inner condition, and relation to the Father. Zechariah 12:1 speaks of the Father forming “the spirit of man within him”; the claim is that the human spirit depends on the Father, not that the verse explains its material composition. Job 32:8 associates spirit and the Almighty’s breath with understanding. Daniel 7:15 describes an anxious spirit; Genesis 41:8 likewise reports Pharaoh’s troubled spirit. Those contexts resist a proposed rule that *soul* alone handles feelings while *spirit* alone handles reason.

David’s prayer in Psalm 51:10–12 distinguishes his condition from the Father’s Spirit:

> “renew a right **spirit** within me”; “take not your Holy **Spirit** from me”; “uphold me with a willing **spirit**.”

The repeated English word does not make all three phrases identical. David asks for renewal and help; he also asks that the Father not withdraw his Holy Spirit. Ecclesiastes 12:7 describes spirit returning to the Father at death, while James 2:26 says the body apart from the spirit is dead. These texts connect spirit with life and death without specifying a complete conscious itinerary after death. The [human spirit article](https://eternal.family.net.za/creation/human/spirit) also gathers these life, feeling, understanding, and resurrection contexts; none yields a simple “instinct belongs to soul, reason belongs to spirit” division.

### Angelic spirit

Psalm 103:20 addresses the Father’s “**angels**, you mighty ones who do his word.” Micaiah’s vision places “all the **host of heaven** standing beside him” (1 Kings 22:19), then reports:

> “Then a **spirit** came forward and stood before the LORD.”

The scene presents a speaking spirit in a heavenly setting, but does not explicitly equate every angel with that spirit. Hebrews 1:14 calls angels “**ministering spirits**.” As a confirming passage, it makes the angel–spirit connection explicit; Psalm 103:20 and 1 Kings 22:19–22 do not themselves identify that speaker as an angel. None of these passages supplies angelic anatomy.

Mark 1:23 calls an agent confronting Jesus an “**unclean spirit**”; Mark 1:27 reports that such spirits obey him. The passages do not establish that all spirits are angels or that unclean spirits are departed humans. Psalm 104:4 says the Father “makes his **messengers winds**,” which alone cannot settle angels’ composition. Luke 24:39 provides a confirming comparison when the risen Lord says that “a **spirit** does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have”; it is not a materiality rule for every use of *spirit*.

### Collective spirit

Philippians 1:27 calls believers to be:

> “standing firm in one **spirit**, with one mind striving side by side for the faith of the gospel.”

“One spirit” expresses their unity in the stated task. The passage need not posit a single new being made by combining them. In the same verse, “one mind” translates *psychē*, showing how soul and spirit vocabulary can stand together in language about shared purpose. Acts 4:32 similarly says the believers were “of one heart and soul.”

The Father’s Spirit can also rest upon multiple people without becoming a collective creature. In Numbers 11:17, the Father says he will take some of the Spirit on Moses and put it on the elders; Moses’ response in Numbers 11:29 welcomes the prospect that the Father would put his Spirit on all his people. That account concerns the Father’s action among persons, not a group spirit produced by their agreement.

### Spirit of God

Isaiah 42:1 records the Father saying:

> “I have put my **Spirit** upon him.”

The chosen servant receives the Father’s Spirit; the verse does not identify the servant as the Father’s soul or as one of his bodily parts. Numbers 11:17 and 11:29 likewise describe the Father giving his Spirit to empower his people. Exodus 31:3 applies this language to the ability given for skilled work. Calling this the Father at work follows the actions described in those passages; reducing his Spirit to an impersonal wind does not.

John 4:24 states:

> “God is **spirit**, and those who worship him must worship in **spirit** and truth.”

The two occurrences belong to a statement about the Father and true worship, not a claim that every occurrence of *spirit* names a being of the same kind as the Father. Isaiah 42:1 is especially useful here: the Father speaks of both “my soul” and “my Spirit” with different work in the sentence. Neither term supplies a physical formula for him.

Paul’s prayer in 1 Thessalonians 5:23 says:

> “may your whole **spirit** and **soul** and **body** be kept blameless.”

Paul names all three while praying for the person’s wholeness. *Trichotomy* treats body, soul, and spirit as three components; *dichotomy* commonly treats soul and spirit together in contrast with body. These are interpretive models, not definitions established merely by the list. An emphasis on wholeness also must not erase Matthew 10:28’s distinction between soul and body. Mark 12:30 calls for love with heart, soul, mind, and strength; reading that list as a call involving the whole person is an inference about its purpose, not proof that its terms always mean the same thing. Hebrews 4:12 speaks of dividing soul and spirit, but as a confirming passage it cannot bear a complete theory of human composition by itself.

Resurrection language supplies another boundary. First Corinthians 15:42 says:

> “What is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable.”

Verse 44 adds:

> “It is sown a **natural body**; it is raised a **spiritual body**.”

Paul describes a resurrection contrast, not separate spiritual bodies already possessed by every living human or animal. “Spiritual” identifies the raised body in that argument; the adjective alone does not specify its material composition. Verse 45 quotes Adam becoming “a **living being**” (with “a living soul” noted as the Greek wording) and calls the last Adam “a **life-giving spirit**.” The comparison concerns Adam and the risen Christ in Paul’s resurrection argument. It is not a rule that every use of *soul* means natural body or every use of *spirit* means an immaterial person.

### Conclusion

Biblical usage supports overlapping meanings for [soul in living creatures](#animal-soul) and [spirit in living creatures](#animal-spirit), while the [quoted examples](#biblical-soul-usage-with-quoted-examples) and [comparison](#comparison) preserve distinctions where context makes them. The [Father’s soul](#soul-of-god), the [Father’s Spirit](#spirit-of-god), and [personal spirits in narratives](#angelic-spirit) must not be forced into one creaturely definition.

**Research limit:** These passages establish their stated uses and contrasts; they do not provide a universal materiality test for unseen beings or a complete account of every creature’s postmortem experience.
