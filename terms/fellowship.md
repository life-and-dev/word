# Fellowship

Fellowship means sharing or participation. John describes fellowship with [the Father](https://ofgod.info), his [Son](https://son.ofgod.info), and one another, and connects it with walking in the light (1 John 1:3–7).

## Definitions

**Fellowship means sharing or participation.** Its character depends on what is shared and with whom: Scripture describes fellowship with God, partnership in the gospel, and participation in idolatrous worship (1 John 1:3; Philippians 1:5; 1 Corinthians 10:20–21). Fellowship is therefore not simply another word for friendly conversation.

| Term             | Main emphasis                                | Relationship to fellowship                                                                                                                                              |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Fellowship**   | Sharing or participating in something        | Context identifies the relationship, purpose, or activity shared.                                                                                                       |
| **Friendship**   | Personal affection, trust, and companionship | Friendship can accompany fellowship, but believers can partner in the gospel without being close personal friends.                                                      |
| **Conversation** | Exchanging words                             | Conversation can express fellowship, but speaking with someone does not, by itself, mean sharing their beliefs or participating in their conduct.                       |
| **Worship**      | Honoring and revering a deity                | Worship can express fellowship through shared participation, but fellowship is not worship. Fellowship also includes other shared activities such as practical support. |

These distinctions appear in concrete biblical examples:

- **Ordinary hospitality is not necessarily religious participation.** Paul permits accepting an unbeliever’s dinner invitation, while forbidding participation in the table of demons (1 Corinthians 10:20–21, 27).
- **Fellowship can take material form.** Romans 15:26 uses the Greek word *koinōnia*, elsewhere translated “fellowship,” for a **“contribution”** to poor believers. Sharing resources can express fellowship without a face-to-face conversation.
- **Fellowship and worship belong together without being identical.** Acts 2:42 lists fellowship alongside the apostles’ teaching, breaking bread, and prayers. The believers also share resources according to need (Acts 2:44–45).
- **Fellowship with God involves more than words.** Claiming fellowship while walking in darkness contradicts the truth; John connects fellowship with walking in the light (1 John 1:6–7).

The expression **“shared life”** can summarize Christian fellowship, but it is an explanatory phrase—not a direct biblical definition. Here it means relationship with [the Father](https://ofgod.info), [his Son Jesus Christ](https://ofgod.info/christ), and fellow believers, expressed through faithful conduct and practical partnership. **Conversation can express that relationship, but fellowship is not limited to conversation.**

## Greek and Syriac terms

In the preserved Greek New Testament, [κοινωνία (G2842)](https://biblehub.com/greek/2842.htm) can mean fellowship, participation, or sharing. Related [κοινωνέω (G2841)](https://biblehub.com/greek/2841.htm) means to share or participate. [κοινωνός (G2844)](https://biblehub.com/greek/2844.htm) denotes a partner or participant. Each passage identifies what is shared.

[Syriac is an Aramaic dialect](https://syriaca.org/about-syriac.html), and a Syriac dictionary glosses [ܫܘܬܦܘܬܐ](https://sedra.bethmardutho.org/lexeme/get/3161) as “fellowship”.

## Covenant life in Israel

Deuteronomy 12:5–7 directs Israel to bring offerings to the chosen place, eat before the Father, and rejoice with their households. That rejoicing includes children, servants, and Levites (Deuteronomy 12:12). The third-year tithe provides food for Levites, sojourners, orphans, and widows (Deuteronomy 14:28–29). These commands give concrete examples of shared worship and provision, not a definition of a later Greek word. Israel is also commanded to love the Father (Deuteronomy 6:5), its neighbors (Leviticus 19:18), and the sojourners among them (Leviticus 19:34).

## Fellowship in the New Testament

Although Jesus departed and is promised to return (Acts 1:9–11), the Father calls believers into fellowship with His Son (1 Corinthians 1:9).

John says the apostles proclaim what they saw and heard so **others can share fellowship** with the Father and Son (1 John 1:3–7). That relationship is expressed by walking in [the light](https://kingdom.ofgod.info/light), keeping Jesus’s teaching, and showing [love](love.md) through care for people in need (1 John 2:3–6; 3:16–24). It does not depend on physical proximity to Jesus.

Paul calls the cup and bread a sharing in Jesus’s blood and body (1 Corinthians 10:16–21). The meal remembers and **proclaims his sacrifice** until he comes (1 Corinthians 11:23–26). It does not mean believers physically [enter inside Jesus](../semantics/en.md). Fellowship also takes shape in gospel work (Philippians 1:5), **sharing his sufferings** (Philippians 3:10), and **giving to believers in need** (Romans 15:26). Members of [Christ’s body](https://kingdom.ofgod.info/christ/body.md) have distinct gifts yet **care for one another** (1 Corinthians 12:12–27). These are all examples of "fellowship".

Nobody is replacing Jesus. [Jesus remains its head](https://son.ofgod.info/shema/jesus-today.md#christ-remains-the-head) (Colossians 1:18).

## Shared participation and ordinary hospitality

The [one bread](/bible/metaphors/bread) expresses believers’ participation in Christ as [one body](/kingdom/body) (1 Corinthians 10:14–17). Fellowship with [the Father](/god/father) and [his Son](/god/son) also joins believers to one another as they walk in [light](/bible/metaphors/light) (1 John 1:3–7).

An ordinary meal with an unbeliever is permitted (1 Corinthians 10:27). An idol-sacrifice meal is different: joining that worship means sharing in what the sacrifice represents (Exodus 34:15; 1 Corinthians 10:18–22). Such idolatry—not ordinary hospitality—provokes the Father to jealousy (Exodus 34:14–15; 1 Corinthians 10:22). If someone identifies food as sacrificed, abstain for that person’s conscience (1 Corinthians 10:28–29).

Food and drink are not grounds for judgment; the body grows from God by holding fast to its head, [Christ](/god/son/christ) (Colossians 2:16–19). Believers must not enter an unequal yoke with unbelievers (2 Corinthians 6:14–18), and must expose [sin](/bible/concepts/sin) rather than share in unfruitful works of darkness (Ephesians 5:8–13). Paul thanks God for the Philippians’ “**fellowship in the gospel**” (Philippians 1:3–6, NKJV): partnership in the gospel, not every shared meal, marks this fellowship.

## Voice and conversation

John 10:27 joins hearing Jesus’s voice with following him. It does not specify that every disciple must hear private audible or inner speech. The **apostles’ witness communicates his teaching** to later believers (1 John 1:3, 2:3–6). Following that teaching and serving his people express fellowship while Jesus is bodily absent. Neither conversation alone nor a private voice is given as its requirement.

## Conclusion

Israel’s [shared worship and provision](#covenant-life-in-israel) give concrete background; [Greek and Syriac terms](#greek-and-syriac-terms) describe sharing, not physical location. [Fellowship with the Son](#fellowship-in-the-new-testament) is made known through the apostles’ witness and expressed in obedience, the meal, and shared service—not bodily co-presence. [Shared participation](#shared-participation-and-ordinary-hospitality) distinguishes worship and gospel partnership from ordinary hospitality. [Hearing his voice](#voice-and-conversation) is not presented as a required private experience.
